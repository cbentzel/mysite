I grew up in Central Maine. As a teenager, I learned how influential many of the senators from our region were, such as [Margaret Chase Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margaret_Chase_Smith), [George Mitchell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_J._Mitchell), and [Edmund Muskie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Muskie).

These were all very accomplished individuals. But part of the reason for influence may have been the disproportionate power smaller states have in the federal government, particularly in the Senate.

Recently I've been doing a mental activity of - how small could a US State be, and how could that be abused by a party to entrench power? 

## What the Constitution Actually Requires

New states are governed by **Article IV, Section 3**:

> New States may be admitted by the Congress into this Union; but no new State shall be formed
> or erected within the Jurisdiction of any other State; nor any State be formed by the Junction
> of two or more States, or Parts of States, without the Consent of the Legislatures of the
> States concerned as well as of the Congress.

For a state carved out of an existing state, three approvals are needed:

1. **The legislature(s) of the state(s) being carved up.**
2. **Both chambers of Congress**, passing the admission bill.
3. **The President**, by signing it.

Article IV's own text names only the first two — the state legislature and Congress. The
President's role isn't from Article IV at all; it's there because admission is accomplished
through ordinary legislation, which means it goes through the normal Article I, Section 7
presentment process like any other bill. If the President vetoes it, two-thirds of each chamber of
Congress can override.

Once admitted, a state has the same constitutional status as every other state (*Coyle v. Smith*,
1911) — Congress can't attach permanent second-class conditions to it. And the Constitution's own
text gives every state, new or old, the same baseline representation regardless of population:

- **Two Senators**, guaranteed by Article I, Section 3, and further locked in by Article V, one of
  the Constitution's two original express limits on the amendment power. (The other — barring any
  change to the slave-trade and capitation-tax clauses before 1808 — expired that year, as
  designed.) Equal Senate suffrage is the one still standing, and it can't be amended away from a
  state without that state's own consent.
- **At least one Representative**, guaranteed by Article I, Section 2, regardless of population.
- **Electoral votes** guaranteed by Article II, Section 1: House seats plus Senate seats, so a
  minimum of three regardless of how small the state is.

Does the Constitution say anything else about what a state needs to look like? Article IV gives
Congress broad discretion over admission. The one textual guardrail is the *Guarantee Clause* in
Article IV, Section 4, which requires a "Republican Form of Government" — though courts have
generally left it to Congress to judge what that means, rather than enforcing it as a checklist.
Beyond that, there is no minimum physical size threshold — current states span a roughly 400x range
by total area, from Rhode Island at the low end to Alaska at the high end — and no minimum
population threshold, where the range is about 67x (per 2025 Census Bureau estimates), from Wyoming
to California.

So historically, how small could a state actually get?

## The Practical Floor Has Always Been Political, Not Legal

Congress has never actually tested the constitutional floor, but it has come close, and every
time it did, the population requirement it applied was a policy choice made in that admission
act — not something the Constitution demanded.

| State | Admitted | Population at admission | Why it went through |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nevada | 1864 | ~40,000 | [Rushed through](https://www.history.com/this-day-in-history/the-u-s-congress-admits-nevada-as-the-36th-state) before the 1864 election to help re-elect Lincoln and secure ratification of the 13th Amendment; silver wealth made the case politically easier to sell. |
| West Virginia | 1863 | Several hundred thousand — population wasn't the issue | Carved out of Virginia during the Civil War using the consent of the "Restored Government of Virginia" — a rump, Unionist legislature representing the state's western counties, not the full prewar Virginia legislature. |
| Wyoming | 1890 | ~60,000 | Roughly the informal population bar Congress was applying at the time (about one House district's worth). |

West Virginia is the most useful precedent here, because it's the one case where the *consent of
the state being divided* was itself contested. Virginia never consented in any ordinary sense —
its legitimate government had seceded and joined the Confederacy. The "consent" came from a
breakaway legislature that controlled only the loyal western counties, and Lincoln himself doubted
the bill's constitutionality but signed it anyway under wartime pressure.

Virginia did eventually get its day in court, but not on the question that matters here. The 1870
case, *Virginia v. West Virginia*, decided whether two additional counties — Berkeley and
Jefferson — had properly joined the new state after the fact; the Court upheld the transfer based
on an 1866 congressional resolution consenting to it. The bigger question — whether West Virginia's
*original* 1863 separation from Virginia was constitutional at all — was never squarely presented
to the Supreme Court and has never been decided. That's the more useful fact here: not that a court
looked at a shaky secession and waved it through, but that a state can be carved out of a live
rebellion, exist for over 160 years, and have the central question of its own legitimacy simply
never come up for decision. Once a new state exists and Congress has admitted it, nobody with
standing to challenge it seems to have much appetite for trying.

## Could a One-Person State Exist?

Nothing in the text stops it. There is no enumerated population minimum anywhere in Article IV.
The only two checks are:

1. **The Guarantee Clause**, which *Luther v. Borden* treats as non-justiciable — so it's mainly a
   political argument Congress could raise against admission, not a bar courts have historically
   been willing to enforce.
2. **The ordinary legislative process** — majorities in the House and Senate, plus a presidential
   signature (or veto override). In modern practice, that also means surviving a Senate filibuster,
   which requires 60 votes unless the chamber carves out an exception the way it has for some
   other categories of business.

So the honest answer is: there's no textual bar, and no court has ever been willing to enforce
one — which is not quite the same claim as "a one-person state is settled, affirmative law."
Nobody has tried it, so nobody actually knows. What's clear is that it would take Congress
*wanting* to do something transparently disproportionate — a one-person state would still receive
two Senators and a House seat, the same baseline representation as California's 39 million
residents. That's not a loophole anyone stumbles into; it's a decision Congress would have to make
on purpose, with the political fallout in full view.

**Could it stay a one-person state?** Not by any *governmental* mechanism. Admission doesn't
freeze a state's population, and nothing lets a state wall off its own citizenship: the Privileges
and Immunities Clause and the Fourteenth Amendment's right to travel guarantee that any U.S.
citizen can move to any state and immediately become one of its citizens, vote included. In
*Saenz v. Roe* (1999) the Supreme Court struck down even a one-year *waiting period* before new
California residents got equal welfare benefits, holding that a new arrival's citizenship in their
new state is instant rather than earned over time.

But that binds the *state*, not a private landowner, and the difference turns out to be the whole
ballgame. Draw the state's boundary as exactly one person's house and lot and there is no public
land to arrive on. *Saenz* guarantees equal treatment once you are a resident; it does not create
a right to be physically present somewhere you have no lawful right to be, and domicile — presence
plus intent to remain — is not something a court will credit when the presence is ongoing
trespass. Nor is the usual squatter's escape hatch available, because the microstate writes its
own property and trespass law and its own courts decide residency disputes: the adverse possession
period is however many centuries the sole legislator says it is. Exclusion by deed is far more
durable than exclusion by statute, precisely because the Constitution polices the latter and
ignores the former.

The leaks that remain are about ownership changing hands, not about anyone moving in:

- **The parcel is alienable.** It can be sold, inherited, or split in a divorce, and two Senate
  seats are worth immeasurably more than a house — so each microstate is a purchasable asset with
  a discoverable price, and devices meant to lock ownership down in perpetuity (trusts, entails)
  erode against the rule against perpetuities. One owner might hold the line. Across a million of
  them, over decades, the defection rate is not zero.
- **The household votes, eventually.** A spouse, an adult child, a live-in caretaker, a houseguest
  who stays — anyone the owner admits and who intends to remain becomes a resident, though not
  instantly: *Dunn v. Blumstein* (1972) struck down long durational residency requirements for
  voting but blessed roughly 30 days as the outer edge of what a state may require to administer an
  election. So the electorate is "whoever the owner admits, after about a month," which is still not
  the same thing as one person, and a bad divorce could flip a Senate seat within a single election
  cycle.
- **Federal eminent domain, conditionally.** Washington can condemn land inside any state, and
  *Evans v. Cornman* (1970) held that residents of a federal enclave must be allowed to vote in
  the surrounding state. But that holding turned on Maryland exercising general criminal and taxing
  jurisdiction over the enclave's residents, so the lever works only if the microstate retains that
  jurisdiction — a cession of exclusive federal jurisdiction would likely cut the other way.
- **Nobody can enforce any of it.** A state whose entire police force is one person has trespass
  law in the sense that a sign on a fence is law. Enforcement means borrowing federal marshals or
  the neighboring state's deputies, and the microstate controls neither.

The property trick also creates a constitutional problem of its own, and it is a much better
version of the Guarantee Clause objection than "one person cannot be a republic." If the boundary
is drawn so that the state's electorate is determined by a single landowner's right to exclude,
then the franchise has become an incident of private property. That isn't a republican form of
government; it's a proprietary fief. And unlike a vague complaint that a tiny state feels
unrepublican, it states a standard a court could actually apply — which matters, because
everything that follows depends on the assumption that no court will intervene.

That assumption is shakier than it looks. *Luther v. Borden* treats Guarantee Clause questions as
a political question beyond judicial reach, but courts have a track record of reaching the same
kind of claim by a side door instead of the front one. *Baker v. Carr* (1962) made legislative
districting justiciable by recasting what could have been a Guarantee Clause complaint as an Equal
Protection claim instead, and in *New York v. United States* (1992) the Court expressly reserved
the possibility that "perhaps not all claims under the Guarantee Clause present nonjusticiable
political questions." Recasting the claim under a different doctrine is exactly the move available
here too — which is what the next argument does.

There's a second, more specific doctrine pointing the same direction: *Shelley v. Kraemer* (1948)
held that a court *enforcing* a private exclusionary arrangement — there, a racially restrictive
covenant — is itself state action reachable under the Fourteenth Amendment, even though the private
agreement alone would have been beyond the Constitution's reach. A microstate's own courts
enforcing trespass to keep a would-be voter out is the same structure: private exclusion, backed by
judicial enforcement, which is exactly the seam *Shelley* found. The caveat is that *Shelley* has
been kept almost pointedly narrow in the decades since — taken literally, it would turn ordinary
enforcement of any private exclusion into a constitutional question, so courts have confined it
close to its facts. It's a second plausible line of attack, not a settled one.

Which leaves whoever is designing this scheme with a choice, and no good version of it. Draw the
microstates as private parcels and they are migration-proof, but you have handed a court the
cleanest Guarantee Clause hook anyone has articulated. Draw them as ordinary territory with public
roads and public land and the fief problem evaporates — but so does the power to exclude, and a
state with one voter and a public road is the easiest jurisdiction in the country to flip. The two
vulnerabilities are the same design decision seen from opposite ends.

**What happens if the sole resident dies?** Usually the same thing that happens to any house: it
passes to an heir, who inherits the property and with it the state's entire population and
electorate. That's the mundane answer, and it follows from the design — a state built on a parcel
is inherited like a parcel, which means its politics are only ever as stable as its estate
planning. Two Senate seats would make for remarkable will contests.

The genuinely unresolved case is when nobody inherits. No U.S. state has ever had its population
reach zero, and the Constitution has no mechanism for "un-admitting" one: *Texas v. White* (1869)
held the Union to be a compact of "indestructible States," so a state cannot be dissolved
unilaterally by anyone, Congress included. The state would simply continue to exist with nobody in
it — no governor to appoint a replacement Senator, no legislature, nobody to hold an election.
There's even a small knot in the escheat rules, under which property left with no heirs passes to
the state: here, the state is the decedent. A careful admission act would head all of this off by
providing that the state's offices need not be held by its sole *resident* — a governor,
legislature, and judiciary existing on paper, staffed by people who live elsewhere, the way a shell
company has officers. That such a clause would be necessary is the tell. The closest real-world
analogue, uninhabited U.S. possessions like Palmyra Atoll, are deliberately kept as federally
administered territories rather than states, precisely to avoid handing self-government and two
Senate seats to a place with nobody to self-govern.

## How a Same-Party Trifecta Could Abuse This

This is where the historical curiosity turns into a real, if extreme, strategic tool. Suppose one
party controls the House, the Senate, and the Presidency — a trifecta — *and* also controls the
legislature of one large state. Article IV, Section 3 gives that combination a path that a
federal trifecta alone does not have:

1. **The state legislature votes to divide itself** into many new states, drawing the new
   boundaries to concentrate its own party's voters into as many small states as possible.
2. **One leftover state absorbs everyone else** — including the bulk of the opposition party's
   voters — no matter how large that remainder state ends up being.
3. **Congress admits all the new states** in one or more ordinary statutes, signed by the
   President.

The payoff is Senate seats, not House seats. Because every state gets exactly two Senators
regardless of population, splitting one large state into, say, ten friendly small states turns 2
Senate seats into 20, while the opposition-heavy remainder state — even if it holds most of the
original population — is still capped at 2. The same math compounds in the Electoral College,
since electoral votes track House-plus-Senate seats.

This is not a purely theoretical thought experiment; versions of it have been floated seriously:

- **["Six Californias"](https://www.npr.org/2018/06/13/619525289/california-or-cali-three-nia-proposal-to-split-state-will-be-on-ballot-in-novemb)**
  and the later "Cal 3" ballot measure proposed splitting California into multiple states,
  explicitly discussed in terms of the Senate seats it would create.
- **["Pack the Union"](https://harvardlawreview.org/print/vol-133/pack-the-union-a-proposal-to-admit-new-states-for-the-purpose-of-amending-the-constitution-to-ensure-equal-representation/)**,
  a Harvard Law Review piece, proposed carving many small new states out of federal territory for
  the explicit purpose of amending the Constitution. Note that new states supply *both* halves of
  Article V: Congress proposes an amendment by two-thirds of each chamber, and the **states**
  ratify it by three-fourths.
- Analysts have periodically noted that splitting a large state — New York has come up as often
  as California — would shift the Senate by exactly the number of new states created, in either
  party's favor depending on who controls the legislature doing the splitting.

### A More Realistic Version: Many 75,000-Person States

Nothing in the math above caps the number of new states at ten, and the one-person-state thought
experiment from earlier suggests an obvious way to push it further. But going straight to one-person
states is also an easy scenario to dismiss as fantasy. A subtler version avoids that problem by
staying well inside a size Congress has already treated as an ordinary state: Wyoming was admitted
with about 60,000 people. Call the target 75,000 — comfortably above any historical admission
threshold, and, just as importantly, comfortably above the 30,000-person ratio in Article I,
Section 2, so none of the apportionment math below runs into a wall.

Suppose a Democratic-controlled trifecta, with a Democratic-controlled California legislature,
identifies roughly half of California's population — about 19.5 million people, spread across
whichever counties and precincts vote most reliably Democratic — and draws that territory into
states of 75,000 people each:

19,500,000 ÷ 75,000 ≈ **260 new states.**

The other half of California — every Republican, plus anyone not sorted into a friendly district —
becomes the one remainder state, same as in the general mechanism above. (The party labels are
interchangeable; a Republican trifecta holding the Texas or Florida legislature runs the identical
play with the names swapped.)

Run the same three numbers:

- **Senate:** 260 new states × 2 Senators = **520 new Senate seats**, replacing the 2 the whole
  state used to have. The remainder state, no matter how many people end up living there, still
  gets 2.
- **House:** 260 new states each need a minimum of one Representative — 260 new seats. That's real,
  but nowhere near a constitutional problem: the country's total population (roughly 349 million)
  caps the *entire* House, nationally, at about 11,600 seats under the 30,000-person ratio, and
  today's House uses only 435 of that room. Adding 260 more spends a sliver of headroom that's
  already there.
- **Electoral College:** 260 × 3 = **780 new electoral votes**, against roughly 54 for the whole of
  California today (52 House seats plus 2 Senate seats).

That's a smaller number than the one-person extreme, but it's already a bigger distortion of the
Senate than anything in American history, and it comes with none of the extreme version's
problems. It doesn't need the property trick to survive migration, because 75,000
people spread across real neighborhoods isn't a fragile one-vote monopoly that a single new
resident can flip — it's an ordinary political majority, the kind that already exists inside
plenty of state legislative districts today. It doesn't strain the Guarantee Clause the way a fief
built around one landowner's front door does — a state of 75,000 people with its own government
looks, on paper, exactly like the states already in the Union. And it doesn't touch the
apportionment ceiling at all.

There's a further wrinkle worth noticing. Replacing 1 state with 261 (260 friendly states plus the
remainder) brings the total number of states in the Union to 310. Holding 260 of those 310 state
legislatures is about 84% — past the three-fourths of all states that Article V requires to ratify
a constitutional amendment, using nothing but the split of this one state. Whatever else this
maneuver is, it isn't only a Senate play.

### The Theoretical Ceiling

It's worth asking how far the one-person version from earlier in this piece could theoretically go,
if only to find the actual edge of the idea rather than an invented one.

The Senate side has no edge: nothing in the Constitution bounds how many states can each hold two
Senators, because Senate seats were never tied to a population ratio in the first place. The House
side does have one, and it's a hard one. Every state's guaranteed minimum of one Representative,
combined with the rule that the House "shall not exceed one for every thirty Thousand" people
*nationally*, means the country cannot have more total states than (total U.S. population ÷
30,000) — at today's population, roughly **11,600 states, existing 50 included**. That is the real
ceiling on this entire idea, whether the new states are built around one person or 75,000: not a
million, not an invented number, but a specific, calculable limit falling directly out of Article
I, Section 2. Even at that ceiling the Senate math is staggering — something like 23,200 seats, if
the whole country were carved to the constitutional maximum — but it is *finite*, and any one
state's split would exhaust only a fraction of the room before the national wall came into view.

As for the Article V power the 75,000-person version already stumbles into: at a large enough
scale, most amendments — abolishing the Electoral College, for instance — face no special obstacle
once a party holds three-fourths of however many states exist; that's just an ordinary use of the
amendment power once the votes are there. The one thing that supermajority can't straightforwardly
do is touch the Senate itself. Article V separately protects "equal Suffrage in the Senate" against
being taken from any state without that state's own consent, and whether that protection can be
amended away using Article V's own procedure — the amendment power being used against a limit on
itself — is a genuinely unresolved question in constitutional theory, not a settled yes.

How permanent is any of this? More than it should be, but not because the Constitution forecloses a
reversal. Migration won't undo the one-person version, for the property reasons above, and the
75,000-person version was never vulnerable to migration in the first place. But the same Article IV
sentence that lets states be carved up also lets a state "be formed by the Junction of two or more
States" on the same two consents, so the pieces can in principle be reassembled whenever the
politics allow. What actually makes the maneuver durable isn't that it's a one-way ratchet in
constitutional terms — it's that the party holding hundreds of new Senate seats is also the party
whose consent any reversal would require.

## What Actually Blocks This From Happening Overnight

- **The real blocker is the reaction, not the procedure.** Take the 75,000-person version seriously
  for a moment: roughly 19.5 million people — the entire other half of California — would be told,
  permanently and by arithmetic rather than by any election they could ever win, that they have no
  path to influence the Senate, the House, or the Presidency again. That isn't a policy defeat to absorb gracefully; it's the
  textbook precondition for a secession movement. West Virginia is the nearest precedent, but it
  needs stating carefully — the western counties were Unionists who broke away because *Virginia*
  had left the Union, not because they felt shut out of the federal compact. What they refused to
  accept was being permanently outvoted inside their own state, and that is the parallel that
  applies here: it is precisely the position the remainder state's population would be placed in,
  except with no exit that the rest of the country would recognize as lawful. The deterrent isn't
  that civil conflict would somehow prevent the bill from passing — a consequence is not a
  procedural obstacle. It's that the legislators who would have to vote for it can see the
  consequence coming in advance, and a Senate majority bought at the price of a secession crisis
  is not obviously a majority worth having.
- **You need the same party controlling the House, Senate, and Presidency, plus that state's own
  legislature, at the same time.** That's a real requirement, but not a rare one — a party's
  biggest, safest states tend to already be under its unified control whenever it also holds the
  federal trifecta. Texas and Florida for Republicans, California and New York for Democrats, have
  been reliably one-party states for decades.
- **Whether the state's own legislators are voting against their own interest is less clear than
  it looks.** Splitting one state into ten doesn't just eliminate 2 Senate seats and 1
  governorship — it creates 20 Senate seats, 10 governorships, and 10 full sets of legislative
  leadership where there used to be one. For an ambitious state legislator, that's a bigger lottery
  of offices, not a smaller one. The real friction isn't self-sacrifice; it's that only a fraction
  of the people voting for the split can actually win one of the new seats, so the plan only holds
  together if party leadership can make credible promises about who gets what before the votes are
  cast.
- **The Senate filibuster, but barely.** Admission acts are ordinary legislation, so absent a rules
  change they need 60 votes. This is the weakest item on the list: the filibuster is a Senate rule
  abolishable by simple majority, as the chamber demonstrated in 2013 and in 2017, and a party
  willing to carve a state into 260 pieces will not be deterred by a procedural norm it can repeal
  in an afternoon. After the first round of admissions it is irrelevant permanently.
- **Equal footing cuts both ways.** *Coyle v. Smith* stops Congress from admitting a state with
  lesser status, but it doesn't stop Congress from admitting many small, equal-status states —
  so it's not a defense against this strategy, just a reason the new states would be "real"
  states with full rights once created.
- **Precedent risk is mutual.** Whichever party does this first hands the other party the same
  playbook the next time the alignment favors them, which has historically made both sides wary
  of being the one to break the norm — the same argument that comes up in modern D.C. and Puerto
  Rico statehood debates.
- **The paperwork is its own mountain.** Even with the votes in hand, 260 new states means 260 new
  federal court districts to define, 260 new election-administration regimes to certify, a Census
  Bureau process built around 50 units now running for 310, and Postal Service and
  federal-benefits systems keyed to state boundaries that no longer resemble anything they were
  built for. None of this stops a Congress that has already decided to do it, but it's real
  friction that would stretch the timeline well past a single legislative session — and it would
  scale up sharply the closer a state pushed toward the theoretical ceiling below.

Almost all of these are political and procedural rather than constitutional, which is the real
takeaway: the floor under "how small can a state be" was never set by the founders. One part of
the question turns out to have a clean, calculable answer rather than an open one — Article I's
ratio caps the entire country at roughly 11,600 states, a hard ceiling that the 75,000-person
version never comes close to and even the one-person extreme can't exceed. What's left genuinely
unresolved is narrower: whether a Guarantee Clause challenge to a state built around a single
landowner's property line could get past *Luther*, and whether the equal-Senate-suffrage clause in
Article V can ever be amended away using the very procedure it constrains. Neither question has an
answer, because nobody has ever tried either one.

None of which is much comfort — and the realistic version doesn't even need those questions
answered to work. Both remain unsettled, both would be litigated for years while the new senators
sat and voted in the meantime, and the lesson of West Virginia is that courts do not unwind states
once they exist. The real floor is whether Congress, at a given moment, wants to say no.
